01 — the problem

The person who has to speak is reading the same mess as everyone else.

Stated as the four Cs: what the problem is, what it costs, why it happens, and what would show it is real.

Clarity

What is the problem?

A duty communications officer has to publish an accurate external statement while an incident is still running. Their only source is the live chat thread. Deciding what in it is a confirmed fact and what is somebody's guess is the whole task — and it is a judgement, not a formatting job.

Consequence

What does it cost?

Two bad options. Take 30–45 minutes to write it carefully, during which the organisation says nothing and the vacuum fills itself. Or move fast and publish a guess. A guess corrected in public stops being a detail and becomes the story — and it names, or blames, a colleague on the way.

Cause

Why does it happen?

Threads are written under pressure. Messages arrive out of order. Timestamps contradict. Figures are retracted an hour later. Someone names a colleague. And a guess repeated three times starts to read like a fact. Nothing in that reduces to a rule you could write as a switch.

Confirmation

What evidence would show it is real?

Four fixture threads carrying 32 planted traps, each one drawn from a way real threads fail: a retraction, a false recovery, a confident wrong diagnosis, a named victim. A tool that merely reformats sails straight through every one of them.

02 — the outcome metric

Time to publishable draft: 112s → 16s.

One number, because it is the one that decides whether the tool gets used at all. A duty officer who has to wait two minutes writes it by hand instead.

Where the time actually went

Not the artefact — the artefact is under 300 words. It was hidden reasoning, plus a whole round-trip spent making the model re-type the thread as JSON just so it could be sorted. Output tokens are the wall clock, so they are what the fix had to move.

reasoning_effortWall clockOutput tokens
default112s8,106
low50s3,137
minimal ← shipped16s1,986

A 4.1× drop in output tokens, and a 7× drop in wall clock at the time of measurement.

The two changes that did it

  • 1 Parse the thread on the server. parseThread reads <time> <speaker>: <text> in about twenty lines of pure JavaScript, sorts the events, and hands them to the model ordered. That removes an entire model round-trip — the run collapses from two rounds to one.
  • 2 Cap the reasoning, not the answer. reasoning_effort drops to minimal. The judgement quality is unchanged; only the invisible deliberation is cut.

The guardrail on this number

A faster setting that got dumber is not a faster app. All 32 traps still hold at minimal — which is the only reason the number is allowed to ship.

What re-measurement showed — and why the metric is stated this way

The 16s above is a recorded benchmark, not a guarantee. Re-running the same fixture three times while writing this page returned 28s, 44s and 58s: the model runs on a shared endpoint whose speed is not the app's to control. What is the app's to control held steady across all three runs — 1,791, 1,875 and 2,416 output tokens, against 8,106 before the change.

So the honest claim is the narrower one: the work removed roughly four fifths of the tokens the app generates, and wall clock follows that down by whatever factor the endpoint is willing to give on the day. Publishing 16s as a flat promise would have been the same mistake the product exists to prevent — stating something as confirmed that the evidence only supports as observed once.

03 — the riskiest assumption

That a model can be trusted to leave the guess out.

Everything else in this project is recoverable. This one is not: if the model will not reliably keep an unconfirmed cause out of a public statement, the tool is worse than useless — it publishes confident, wrong statements faster than a human could.

How it was tested

Not by reading the output and nodding. Four threads were written specifically to break it, each trap documented in a table with the behaviour it demands. They are the demo input and the eval set: change the prompt, re-run all four, check every trap still holds.

The sharpest trap in the set

Line 16 of the thread

10:52 SecOps: no evidence of exfiltration at
this point. still reviewing sign-in logs

A formatter reaches for the fluent paraphrase. It is one word away and it is a different claim — the difference between what was looked for and what was found. This is the only trap in the set that a confident, entirely wrong answer would sail straight through.

The failure this is watching for

× “No data was breached.”

What the app actually wrote

✓ “To date there is no evidence of mailbox access or data exfiltration.”

Verbatim from a live run of this fixture. The hedge survives into the published sentence: absence of evidence is never upgraded into evidence of absence.

The fixtures found real bugs

Writing the 59-line overnight thread exposed two defects nothing else had: parseThread was reading java.sql.SQLTransientConnectionException: as a speaker, and normaliseAndSortEvents was sorting the entire evening after midnight. Both are now covered by tests. Long fixtures earn their keep.

And the deterministic half is tested

26 tests, 26 passing, over the pure functions in src/tools.js — clock parsing across 2.32pm/14:32/0645Z, midnight rollover, timestamp clashes, stack traces, wrapped lines. No network, no DOM. The more the pure code does, the less the model has to.

04 — the experience

Built for someone reading it under pressure.

One screen. Paste, or start from a worked example. Every design decision below is a constraint on the output, not a decoration on the page.

The caps are load-bearing

  • → Timeline: at most 8 rows. The cap forces the model to merge messages into events rather than transcribe them. It is also what stopped it writing “a Finance staff member said she entered her password” — an identifying detail that had survived the no-names rule.
  • → Holding: 120 words. Internal: 150. Being brief is part of the task, not a concession to it.

Model output is treated as hostile

Statement text, timeline rows and status values all originate from the model, so the page renders every one of them with textContent and createElement — never innerHTML. A status value outside the enum falls back to unknown rather than styling whatever string arrived.

05 — the build

The response shape is guaranteed by where the run stops.

A single Cloudflare Worker. The interesting decision is that no code anywhere parses model prose — there is no free-text path out of the loop at all.

The fast path — one round

01 POST /draft { thread } Up to 20,000 characters.
↓
02 parseThread → normaliseAndSortEvents Pure functions. No model involved. Ordered events, plus notes on anything that looked wrong.
↓
03 model judges the ordered events Only one tool is offered, so there is no round-trip left to spend.
↓
04 emit_incident_comms( … ) The loop returns this call's arguments. That is the response.

When a paste cannot be parsed the model extracts the messages itself and calls the sort tool first — two rounds, slower, works on anything. Both paths end at the same tool call, so nothing downstream cares which one ran.

Why that guarantees the shape

emit_incident_comms has no implementation. It is a schema and nothing else, and that schema is the response contract. Because runDraft terminates by returning the call's arguments, an unvalidated shape cannot reach the UI — there is no prose to mis-parse, and no second-guessing a half-formed answer.

tool_choice is deliberately left on auto. Forcing the emit tool would stop the sort tool ever running on the fallback path. Tool availability, not tool_choice, is what constrains the fast path.

One rule the sort function follows

It reports, it never resolves. A timestamp clash or an unreadable time comes back as a note for the model to surface, and no event is ever dropped — an unreadable time keeps its original wording and sorts to the end. A missing message is worse than an unsorted one, and the entire product claim is that the app does not quietly invent certainty.

Stack

Cloudflare Workers · vanilla ES modules, no framework, no build step · node:test · an OpenAI-compatible chat-completions endpoint, swappable at one base URL. The API key lives in a Cloudflare secret and never in the repo.

06 — the extra-knowledge build

Benchmarking the endpoint, instead of guessing at it.

The app was slow and the obvious move was to swap the model. That instinct was wrong, and the only way to find out was to build a measurement habit and run it.

What the measurements taught

  • → Latency varies by more than 5× across one endpoint. Model choice is a real lever — just not the biggest one.
  • → “Flash” in a model name predicts nothing. glm-5.3-flash was the slowest of the set, at six times the speed of the fastest. Re-measure rather than assume.
  • → The bigger lever was not the model at all. Swapping models bought at best 5×. Cutting reasoning_effort and removing a round-trip bought 7× — on the same model.
  • → Log the number that is the wall clock. Output tokens, on every call. A change that looks like a speed-up is only real if that number moved — which is how the 8,106 → 1,986 drop was confirmed rather than felt.
  • → Fast is not free. kimi-k3 never populated openQuestions across two runs. Speed and judgement have to be checked together, which is what the trap table is for.

Evidence the build worked

The findings are written into CLAUDE.md as a standing rule for whoever touches this next: if the output looks weak, raise reasoning_effort before you change the model — it is the cheaper knob and the one with the larger effect. Either way, re-run all four fixtures, because the traps are the only thing that tells you whether a faster setting got dumber. The measurement did not just produce a number; it produced the process that protects it.
